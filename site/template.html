Every figure here is a total return: the change in price plus every distribution paid, reinvested in the fund. That holds for the covered-call funds and for the index funds alike, so the high distributions are fully counted. The index fund holds the same market without selling calls on it. The difference is what the option strategy cost, or earned, over the period.

    What this site does not show

    • No distribution yield. The filings used here do not carry distributions per share, and a current yield needs daily prices.
    • Nothing recent. Funds report monthly, but the SEC makes only the report for the last month of each fiscal quarter public, about two months later. Each fund's figures are as of its own report date.
    • US-registered funds only. European (UCITS) funds do not file this form.
    • No strike for funds that use notes. Where the call exposure sits inside equity-linked notes, the filing names each note, its issuer and its size, not the option terms.
    • No judgement on any fund. The site reports what was filed. It does not forecast and does not rank.

    How the figures are made

    • Monthly returns are joined across reports. Each public report carries the three months that end at its date. Reports of successive quarters give an unbroken monthly series.
    • The order of the three months is checked. The form asks for the three monthly returns oldest first. Some fund families file them newest first. For the five funds shown in detail, both readings are compared with the index fund month by month over all reports, and the fund is read newest first when that at least halves the misfit. Every other fund is tested the same way against the three index funds, and the result for each is in funds.json. Returns over whole reports, such as the 12-month figures in the category table, do not depend on the order.
    • Months before a fund existed are dropped, together with the part month in which it started.
    • The comparison uses the months both funds have on file. Fund families report on different cycles, so the last month of a comparison can be earlier than the fund's latest report.
    • Calls written is the number of contracts written, times 100 shares, times the strike, as a share of the fund's net assets. Near 100% means calls were written against the whole portfolio.
    • Strikes are checked against the holding's name. For some index options the strike field of the SEC data set holds only the last digits. Where the filed number is the tail of the strike in the holding's name, the name is used and the table marks it.
    • Net flow is sales plus reinvestments less redemptions. A month in which a filing reports a negative amount for one of these is left out and marked.
    • One share class per fund. Where a report lists several classes, the first class identifier is used.

    How a covered-call fund works

    It owns shares and sells someone else the right to their gains above a set price. The payment it receives for that right is what funds the high distribution. The gains it signed away are the cost.

    By Agisilaos Papadogiannis · Background: High Yield, Capped Gains (JBIS, 2026) · Source: SEC Form N-PORT · code · data

    The trade, one month at a time

    1. Own the shares

    The fund holds a portfolio of shares, often a whole index. Say it is worth 100 today.

    2. Sell a call on them the strike

    The fund sells a call option: the buyer gets every gain above an agreed price, the strike, until the option expires. A strike at today's price gives away all the upside. A strike some way above keeps the first part of a rise.

    3. Collect the premium the income

    The buyer pays for the option up front. This premium is the fund's to keep whatever happens. It is larger when markets are nervous and when the strike is close to today's price.

    4. Repeat

    When the option expires the fund sells the next one. Most funds do this every month, some every week or every day.

    What follows from it

    • The yield is not the return. The distribution is paid largely out of premiums. What the investor ends up with is the total return: the distributions plus the change in the fund's value. A fund can pay 10% a year and return much less, or much more, than that.
    • Rising markets are where the cost appears. In a strong month the shares rise past the strike and the fund keeps only the premium and the rise up to the strike. Over a strong year, twelve capped months add up to a wide gap against the index.
    • Falling markets are only cushioned. The premium offsets the first part of a fall and nothing more. The fund keeps almost all of the downside of the shares.
    • Sharp reversals hurt twice. If shares fall and then recover within the life of a new option, the fund takes the fall and is capped on the recovery.
    • Quiet, flat markets are where it does best. The shares go nowhere, the option expires worthless, and the premium is extra return.

    Three names, two ideas

    The funds on this site carry one of three names. Two of them mean the same trade. The third is a looser label.

    Covered call

    The trade described above: hold the shares, sell calls on them. "Covered" means the fund owns what it has promised, so it can always deliver.

    Buy-write

    The same trade under an older name: buy the shares and write (sell) the call. It is the term used by the index providers whose benchmarks many of these funds follow, so funds that track such an index tend to carry it.

    Premium income

    A description of the aim, income from option premiums, not of a particular trade. Funds with this name vary: some write calls on only part of the portfolio to keep more upside, some choose their own shares instead of an index, some hold notes issued by banks that carry the option inside them, and some sell options that last a single day.

    What to look at in a fund

    • Total return against the index fund over a period with both rising and falling months. Fund by fund shows it for five large funds.
    • How much of the portfolio is covered. Calls written near 100% of net assets give up the most upside for the most income. The category lists the figure for every fund.
    • How far the strike sits above the market, and how often the option is renewed.
    • Who stands behind the options. A listed option is guaranteed by a clearing house. A note depends on the bank that issued it.
    • Where the distribution comes from. Part of it can be a return of the investor's own capital. That is in each fund's annual report and is not on this site.
